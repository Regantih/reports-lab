Unit economics, honestly

Three numbers, three ways they're dressed up, and a small set of identities that tell you which version is real.

The three numbers

Contribution margin is gross profit minus the variable cost of serving a single customer for a single period. Not gross margin. Not adjusted EBITDA. Contribution margin includes payment fees, hosting attributable to that customer, support cost, and anything else that goes up when the customer goes up.

CAC is what you actually paid to acquire that customer — fully loaded sales and marketing for the period, divided by net new logos closed in the same period. If a founder gives you a "blended CAC" that includes inbound free traffic, ask for paid CAC separately.

Payback is CAC ÷ monthly contribution margin. It's the only one of the three that has a unit (months). Anything past 24 months in software is a yellow flag; past 36 is a thesis-level question.

Three ways they're dressed up

  1. Reclassifying CAC as R&D. Engineers building integrations to win a single account. If it scales with the customer, it's CAC.
  2. Cohort cherry-picking. Showing payback for the best month of last year, on a single channel, with a denominator that excludes returning users.
  3. Gross-margin inflation. Excluding hosting that scales with usage, or excluding the support headcount that scales with seats.

The identity check

For a healthy software business, three things should approximately hold:

  • Net revenue retention > 100% if the GTM is land-and-expand.
  • LTV / CAC > 3 once payback is in steady state.
  • Burn multiple = net burn ÷ net new ARR. Below 1.0 is excellent; above 2.0 in 2026 needs a story.

Numbers that don't satisfy these can still be fine — but the founder has to tell you which identity they're not satisfying and why. If they don't volunteer that, they don't know.
