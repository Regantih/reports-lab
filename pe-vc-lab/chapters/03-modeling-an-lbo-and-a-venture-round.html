Modeling an LBO and a venture round

The arithmetic each side of private investing keeps coming back to. Two formulas, two intuitions, no Excel required.

LBO: MOIC and IRR

An LBO is a story told as a single equation: enterprise value is bought at entry, cash flow services debt for a few years, the company is sold at exit, and the equity check is multiplied. The two metrics worth memorising:

  • MOIC = exit equity ÷ entry equity. A 2.0× MOIC means you doubled the money. A 3.0× MOIC over five years is the rough industry yardstick.
  • IRR ≈ MOIC(1/years) − 1. A 3× over 5 years is ≈25% IRR. A 2.5× over 5 is ≈20%. A 2× over 4 is ≈19%.

Two things move the answer: how much debt you put on at entry (more leverage → higher MOIC if the company performs, more bankruptcy risk if it doesn't), and how much the exit multiple compresses (multiple compression eats almost every other source of return).

Venture: pre-money, post-money, dilution

A priced round has three numbers and one identity:

  • Pre-money valuation: what the company is worth before the new money lands.
  • Investment: the new cheque.
  • Post-money valuation = pre-money + investment.
  • New investor's stake = investment ÷ post-money.

If founders own 80% before a $5M raise at a $20M pre, the post is $25M. The new investors take 20%. The founders are diluted to 64% (80% × 80%). This is the only formula you need to argue about a term sheet at 11pm.

Option pool shuffle

An option-pool top-up done before the round comes out of founder dilution, not investor dilution. A 10% post-money pool top-up at the same $20M pre is roughly equivalent to a $2M discount on the pre. This is where most ten-minute "we agree" moments unwind.

Liquidation preferences

A 1× non-participating preference means the investor takes either their money back or their share of the exit, whichever is more. Participating means they take both — money back and the share. Stacked preferences (later rounds paid before earlier ones) compound badly in a down exit. The cap-table tool shows you what the waterfall looks like at three exit prices, which is usually enough to win the argument.
