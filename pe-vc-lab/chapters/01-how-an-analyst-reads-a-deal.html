How an analyst reads a deal

The questions an IC asks, and the order they're asked in. If your memo answers these in this sequence, the meeting is calm. If it doesn't, it isn't.

The five questions

  1. What is the company actually selling, and to whom? A surprising number of memos answer the wrong product or the wrong buyer. Reread the contract terms, not the website.
  2. Why does this exist now? What changed in the world — a regulation, a cost curve, a buyer's budget line — that makes this fundable in 2026 and not in 2018?
  3. What does winning look like in five years? Concrete numbers: revenue, gross margin, share. If you cannot picture the P&L of the winning version, neither can the founder.
  4. What is the smallest experiment that proves it? The next 90 days. If we can't define a falsifiable milestone, we are buying narrative.
  5. How do we lose money? Not "what are the risks" — that's a slide. The actual mechanism. A precondition that fails, a counterparty that walks, a cost that doesn't compress.

Why the order matters

Every question gates the next. There is no point pricing a thesis before you've named the buyer. No point modeling the win before you've named the change. ICs that drift — into team trivia, into market-size theatre — have usually skipped a question.

If you cannot complete questions 1–3 in two paragraphs, the deal is not yet ready for the room. Send the founder a follow-up note instead.
