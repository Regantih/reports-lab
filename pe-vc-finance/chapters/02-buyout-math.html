The Buyout, in Cash.

Behind every leveraged buyout sits a simple mechanical claim: the equity check at close, plus the cashflows in and out across the hold, equals the final return. Everything else is bookkeeping.

A buyout has three moves. One: the sponsor buys the business at an enterprise value that is the product of an EBITDA number and a multiple. Two: they fund the deal partly with equity and partly with debt; the equity is the check the fund writes. Three: over the next five-or-so years, the company generates cash, that cash pays down debt, and at exit the sponsor gets whatever remains after the lenders are repaid.

Sources and uses

The sources column is the money coming into the deal: sponsor equity, term loans, sometimes a revolver and a seller note. The uses column is what it pays for: the purchase price, transaction fees, and (occasionally) a working-capital top-up. The two sides have to balance. If you remember nothing else from PE finance, remember that.

The three drivers of value

An LBO creates equity value in three ways, and only three:

  • EBITDA growth. The company makes more money. Same multiple, bigger base, bigger enterprise value, bigger equity slice.
  • Multiple expansion. The market — or the buyer — pays a higher multiple than you did. This is the most fragile of the three and, perversely, often the largest contributor in a hot market.
  • Debt paydown. Free cash flow services and amortises debt. Less debt at exit means more equity, even if the enterprise value is unchanged.

The LBO solver in the lab decomposes any deal into these three sources. If the bridge says >60% of your return comes from multiple expansion, your underwriting is a bet on the market. Your IC is going to want to know why.

The covenants nobody wants to talk about

Leverage in an LBO is rarely a single number. There are senior, second-lien and unsecured tranches; covenants on net debt to EBITDA; springing covenants on the revolver; and (lately) increasingly aggressive covenant-lite documents that let sponsors run hotter than would have been allowed a decade ago. The solver assumes a single funded-debt number with a single rate. That's a simplification. In a real model, you would split tranches, schedule mandatory amortisation, and compute interest on a quarterly basis.

The honest stress tests

Three sensitivities determine whether an LBO clears the hurdle:

  1. Cash conversion. Every percentage point of EBITDA-to-FCF that you lose to capex, working capital, or interest comes out of your debt-paydown contribution. Run the deal at 50% and at 70%, see what changes.
  2. Exit at entry multiple. If you can't clear the fund hurdle with no multiple expansion, the deal needs a story for why the buyer pays more.
  3. Rates +200bps. Cash interest goes up, FCF goes down, debt at exit is higher. In 2022-23 this killed deals signed in 2021.
