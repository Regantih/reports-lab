The Five Questions Inside the Case.

Every PE/VC interview case is the same five questions in a different costume. Once you can find them, the case answers itself.

Case prompts in private-capital interviews look bespoke. They are not. Strip away the company name and the industry, and the same five questions are underneath, in the same order. The interviewer is not testing your sector knowledge. They are testing whether you can structure thinking under time pressure.

Question one: capital structure

What's the equity check? How much debt is realistic, given the EBITDA and the sector's leverage norms? At what rate, and with what covenants, will lenders fund this? Equity check is an arithmetic move. Leverage capacity is a judgement: 4x for a cyclical industrial, 6x for a stable services business, lower for anything regulated.

Question two: returns

Base, downside, upside. MOIC and IRR for each. The base is the deal as drawn; the downside is the deal with one cylinder failing (no growth, no multiple expansion, or no debt paydown); the upside is the deal where the thesis fully plays out. The interviewer is not looking for precise numbers — they want to see that you know the levers and the order of magnitude. The LBO solver will give you the math; the interview is about whether you can construct the cases without it.

Question three: the value-creation lever

Of the three sources — EBITDA growth, multiple expansion, debt paydown — which is the deal really betting on? The interviewer wants the deal in one sentence: "this is a multiple-arbitrage deal", "this is a growth platform", "this is a roll-up". A deal that is "all three" is usually a deal that hasn't been thought through.

Question four: exit

Who buys this in five years, at what scale, at what multiple, and why would they pay? "A strategic" is not an answer. Which strategic, and what's their motivation — geographic expansion, capability tuck-in, defensive consolidation? "A larger sponsor" is not an answer. Which sponsor — there are about thirty sponsors who write checks in any given size band. Knowing this is half the answer.

Question five: the deal-killer

What's the single thing that, if it happened, makes this a 0.8x return? Not "execution risk". A specific thing: the founder leaves, the largest customer churns, regulation changes, a substitute technology wins. The interviewer is not testing whether you can list a risk; they are testing whether you can identify the one risk that matters more than the others combined.

The structure that wins

Spend the first 90 seconds extracting the numbers. Spend the next 60 seconds answering the five questions in order. Save the last 30 seconds for the punch line: "I would bid / not bid because [the specific reason]." Three minutes, all five questions, ranked. That's a hireable answer. Anything longer is rambling.

What the analyzer does

The case analyzer in the lab is a coaching tool, not a magic wand. Paste a prompt, and it pulls out the numbers and constructs the five questions for that specific case. It also offers an answer scaffold — three paragraphs you can speak from. The point isn't that the scaffold is perfect; it's that the scaffold forces you into the right structure when nerves are scrambling everything else.

Drill it Open the case analyzer. Paste in any case prompt — yours, a friend's, anything from a guidebook. Try answering the five questions out loud in three minutes, before reading the scaffold. Then compare. The gap between what you said and the scaffold is the gap to close.
