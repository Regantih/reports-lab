The Add-Backs That Don't Add Up.

"Adjusted EBITDA" is a story the seller tells the buyer about what the business "really" earns. Some of it is true. Some of it is the seller's hopes.

EBITDA is a calculated metric, not an accounting one. There is no GAAP definition of EBITDA, and there is certainly no GAAP definition of "adjusted EBITDA". Sellers know this, and a Confidential Information Memorandum will typically present a bridge that walks reported EBITDA up to "adjusted EBITDA" through a stack of add-backs. Some of those add-backs are real. The job in diligence is to figure out which.

The eight categories

Most adjustments fall into one of eight categories. The QoE checker in the lab applies the rules below to any bridge you paste in.

  1. Owner-comp normalisation. Most accepted, but only down to a market rate. If the seller is taking a $400k salary in a business where the role would pay $250k, $150k is a legitimate add-back. Anything that takes total compensation to zero is not.
  2. Personal expenses run through the business. Accepted, with documentation. The car, the family travel, the country club, the relative on payroll. Quantify and remove from go-forward run rate.
  3. One-time legal / settlement / restructuring. Accepted if non-recurring. The trick is the word "one-time". If the company has had a "one-time" legal settlement five years in a row, it's recurring. Same for "one-time restructuring".
  4. COVID / pandemic / event-driven. The most abused category. Sellers normalise the bad parts of 2020 (lost revenue, extra cleaning costs) and keep the good parts (PPP loans, supply-chain windfalls). Symmetric treatment is what a QoE will demand.
  5. Foregone revenue / pro-forma growth. Almost always rejected. "We would have made $X if Y hadn't happened" is a story for underwriting, not a number for pro forma EBITDA. Move it to your investment thesis if you believe it.
  6. Run-rate price increases / not-yet-realised cost cuts. Rejected unless the action is taken and the result is demonstrable in the trailing months. "We're going to renegotiate the supply contract next quarter" doesn't count.
  7. Stock-based compensation. Genuinely controversial. Sponsors typically add it back; QoE providers note it; some buyers leave it in. Treat as a flag and align with the buyer's convention before pricing.
  8. Capitalisable software / ERP / R&D. Accepted as one-time when the project is bounded and complete. Rejected when "transformation" is the company's permanent posture.

The QoE provider's job

Quality-of-earnings providers — the Big Four transaction services arms, plus a long tail of boutiques — do two things. First, they validate that reported EBITDA reconciles to the bank statements (cash exists; revenue is real; expenses are recorded). Second, they apply judgement to the adjustments and produce a "QoE-adjusted EBITDA" that buyers anchor pricing to. A buyer who pays based on the seller's adjusted figure, without QoE, is paying for the seller's hopes.

What good looks like

A clean QoE bridge has total adjustments of less than 10-15% of reported EBITDA, evenly split between owner comp, true one-times, and accounting normalisations. A bridge with 50% adjustments is either a turnaround or a sales pitch. The checker lights up bridges that fail this sanity test.
