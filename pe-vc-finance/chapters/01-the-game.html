The Game in Two Numbers.

Private capital is judged on two numbers: MOIC and IRR. Confuse them and you'll mis-price every deal you see.

MOIC — multiple of invested capital — is the simplest possible measure: how many dollars came back per dollar in. A 2.5x MOIC is two-and-a-half dollars out for every one dollar in. It tells you how big the win was. It says nothing about how long it took.

IRR — internal rate of return — closes that gap. It is the annualised return that, compounded across the holding period, takes the equity check up to the exit value. A 2.5x in three years is about a 36% IRR; the same 2.5x in seven years is about 14%. The first one is a great deal. The second one might not even clear the fund's hurdle.

Why both, always

If you only watch MOIC, you reward sponsors for sitting on assets too long. A 3x return looks fantastic until you find out it took twelve years; the LP would have done better in equities. If you only watch IRR, you reward early exits at the cost of scale. A 50% IRR on a $5M check that doubles in eighteen months is gorgeous arithmetic and irrelevant to the fund's results. A $50M check that delivers 2.8x over five years actually moves the needle.

In practice, IRR is the gating number — fund hurdles are quoted as IRR — and MOIC is the satisfaction number. LPs want to see both above the line. The deal team's job is to underwrite to a number they can defend in both.

The arithmetic shortcut

You will need this constantly. The relationship between MOIC and IRR for a single bullet (one cash in, one cash out, no interim flows):

IRR ≈ MOIC^(1/years) − 1

So 2.0x in 4 years ≈ 19%. 2.5x in 5 years ≈ 20%. 3.0x in 5 years ≈ 25%. 4.0x in 5 years ≈ 32%. Memorise the table — you'll use it on every deal.

Where it gets harder

Real deals have interim flows: dividend recaps, follow-on investments, partial exits. The shortcut above breaks; you need a proper IRR. The LBO solver in the lab computes both MOIC and IRR from a full cashflow stream, including amortisation, dividend recaps if you turn them on, and the value-creation bridge that explains where the return came from.
