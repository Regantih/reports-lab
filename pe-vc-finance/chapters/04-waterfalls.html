Whose Money Comes Out First.

Liquidation preferences look like a footnote. They are the line that decides who gets paid when the company sells.

A liquidation preference is the right of preferred shareholders to be paid out before common shareholders in any liquidity event. The mechanics live in three knobs: the multiple (1x, 2x, 3x of invested capital), the participation (whether the preferred also shares the residual after their preference is paid), and — in the participating case — the cap (the total amount, expressed as a multiple of invested capital, beyond which they convert to common).

1x non-participating: the standard

A 1x non-participating preference says: at exit, the preferred either takes their invested capital back or converts to common and shares pro-rata, whichever yields more. This is the founder-friendly default and the most common term in modern VC. At low exits, the preferred takes their preference (their pro-rata share would be smaller). At high exits, they convert to common (their pro-rata share dominates). The convert-to-common breakpoint is the exit value where the two are equal.

2x participating: the founder-killer

A 2x participating preference says: the preferred takes 2x their invested capital, and then shares the residual pro-rata as if they had also converted to common. This is the worst term founders sign and they almost always sign it without realising the price. On a $100M exit with $20M of 2x participating preferred and 70% common, the preferred takes $40M off the top, and then takes 30% of the remaining $60M. The preferred ends up with $58M. The founders take the rest. They thought they sold the company for $100M; they walked away with $42M.

The cap, the only mercy

A capped participating preferred is participating up to a cap — typically 2x or 3x of invested capital — beyond which the preferred converts to common. The cap creates a second breakpoint. At exits below the cap, participation pays better. Above the cap, conversion does. The result is two regimes: a flat region where the preferred takes their full participation, and an upward region where they convert and ride the upside as common.

Stacked preferences

In a multi-round company, every priced round has its own preference, and the preferences usually stack: senior rounds get paid first, then junior rounds, then common. A Series C with a 1x preference, sitting above a Series B with a 1x preference, sitting above a Series A with a 1x preference, can mean the first $40-60M of any exit goes entirely to the preferred. Founders often don't realise their company has to sell for above this amount before they see a dollar.

The waterfall solver in the lab models stacked preferences with multiples, participation, and caps, and runs an exit-value sweep so you can find the breakpoints. Use it before you sign any term sheet with a multiple above 1x or any participation feature.

The convert decision, geometrically

For a single non-participating tranche: convert when as-converted-share > preference. For stacked non-participating preferences, the decision is interactive — converting senior tranches changes the residual pool that junior tranches play in. The solver iterates this to a stable answer; in practice, this is what good cap-table software (and a spreadsheet that took someone an afternoon to build) does for you.
