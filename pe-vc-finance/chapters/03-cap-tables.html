Where Founders Lose Their Company.

A founder who can read a cap table can defend their equity. A founder who reads only the headline pre-money valuation cannot.

The mechanics of a venture round are deceptively simple. There's a pre-money valuation. There's an investment. The post-money is one plus the other. The investor's percentage is the investment divided by the post-money. The founder's percentage falls by the same dilution everyone else suffers. That's the cartoon version, and it's wrong about a quarter of the time, because of one provision: the option-pool top-up.

The option-pool shuffle

Every venture term sheet asks the company to maintain an option pool — typically 10-15% of post-money — to grant to employees over the next 12-18 months. That's reasonable. What's not reasonable is the question of who pays for the top-up.

If the new pool is taken out of the post-money — i.e., everyone shares the dilution — that's the fair version. If the new pool is taken out of the pre-money — i.e., it sits in the cap table before the price-per-share is calculated — the founders pay for the entire thing. The investor still gets their headline percentage; the headline pre-money valuation is preserved; the price-per-share is lower; and the founders' slice silently shrinks.

The cost is real and rarely small. The dilution solver in the lab quantifies it for any specific round. For a typical Series A — $5M on a $20M pre with a 15% target pool — taking the pool pre-money costs founders roughly two percentage points of post-money ownership versus taking it post. That sounds small. Compounded across three more rounds, it isn't.

Why founders accept it anyway

Two reasons. One: the headline pre-money valuation is the number that gets reported to TechCrunch and to the founder's friends. The pre-money pool keeps that number high. Two: founders rarely have the experienced counsel they need at Series A. By Series B and C, they do, but by then the pre-money pool convention is locked in.

The defence is to size the pool to the actual hiring plan, not to "industry standard 15%". If you have a hiring plan for the next twelve months and a clean justification for 8%, you can usually negotiate to that. The sponsor wants the cap table to be clean at Series B; you want to keep your equity. There's room.

SAFEs, notes, and the as-converted view

Most early-stage rounds today happen on convertible instruments — SAFEs and notes — that don't price equity until a future round. They have caps, discounts, MFN provisions. When the priced round eventually closes, the SAFEs convert in at a price determined by their cap or discount, depending on which is more favourable. The right way to model this is to compute the as-converted shares each SAFE represents and add them to the existing investor count before running the round mechanics. The dilution solver doesn't model SAFEs natively, but if you fold them into the existing-investor-shares input, the math works out.

The number that matters at exit

Founder ownership at the time of exit is the number that determines what they actually take home. After three or four rounds, founders typically end up between 10% and 25% — the variance is mostly determined by how aggressively each round was negotiated and whether the option pool was always taken pre-money. Two points per round, compounded across four rounds, is eight points. That's the difference between $20M and $36M on a $200M exit.
