Why This Sector, Why Now.

A sector thesis is not a deck. It is a one-page document that says where the firm will deploy capital, what has to be true for that to work, and what would prove the firm wrong.

Every fund has a thesis. Most funds have a deck. The two are not the same. A deck is what you show LPs at the end of the diligence cycle; a thesis is what you actually use to decide which deals to pursue. A deck is fifty pages of charts and a market-size triangle; a thesis is one page that fits the seven things the firm has to be honest about.

The seven things

  1. Why now. The structural reason this sector is interesting today, that wasn't true three years ago. If your "why now" is "we've always wanted to do this", you don't have a thesis.
  2. The wedge. Where you enter the market, and why incumbents won't follow you there. The wedge is what protects you from the next ten firms that read the same thesis.
  3. Unit economics that have to be true. The LTV/CAC, the gross margin, the payback period that the deal has to hit. Quantified, not described.
  4. Portfolio shape. Check size, number of deals, ownership target. This determines whether the thesis even fits the fund.
  5. Kill criteria. What would make you walk away from the entire thesis — not one deal, but the whole bucket. If you can't name kill criteria, you'll keep deploying capital long after the thesis has rotted.
  6. The non-consensus call. What do you believe that the market doesn't? If your thesis is what the market already believes, the entry valuation will reflect it.
  7. Standing diligence. The three things you'll always check first, in any deal that matches the thesis.

The honesty test

A thesis that survives is one that anybody in the firm could explain to anybody else, in two minutes, and have them point at the riskiest assumption. Hand the canvas to the smartest skeptic in the firm — someone who works in another sector. They should be able to read it once and tell you the load-bearing assumption without asking a follow-up. If they can't, you're hiding something behind generalities.

The kill criteria are the test

Most theses don't get killed; they get gradually relaxed. Year one: "we only do vertical SaaS with NRR > 110%". Year three: "we only do vertical SaaS". Year five: "we do interesting software". Writing the kill criteria down at the start, in numbers, is what stops the slide. When the cohort drops below the threshold, the firm has a hard decision to make instead of a soft drift.

The non-consensus call

Investment returns come from being right when the market is wrong, or being faster when the market is slow. "We back great founders in great markets" is a strategy that any firm can copy by Tuesday. "We believe data accumulation in dispatch software creates a margin advantage that won't show up until year four" is a claim somebody could be wrong about. That's what makes it useful.

Where the canvas lives

The sector thesis canvas in the lab is the seven sections, on one page, with a printable export. It saves locally to your browser. Nothing leaves the page. Use it for one sector at a time, share the export, and revisit it every six months — that's when the kill criteria matter.

Build it Open the canvas. Pick a sector you actually know — even if it's outside private capital. Try to fill in all seven sections in 30 minutes. The friction you feel while doing it is the work.
