The Memo That Wins the Vote.

An investment committee reads forty memos a year. They read the same seven sections in the same order, every time. Get the order right and the deal lands.

Most associates write IC memos backwards. They lead with the company description, walk through the market, build to the financial model, and conclude with the recommendation. By the time the IC reaches the recommendation, the senior partners have already made up their minds — and their minds were made up in the first ninety seconds, on what they read at the top of page one.

Lead with the thesis

The first paragraph is the only paragraph half the room will read carefully. It must contain three things: what the company does, why this is the right entry point, and what the non-consensus call is. If the IC member can repeat your thesis to their spouse over dinner, you've written it well.

Then the math, anchored

Sources and uses. MOIC and IRR. Hold period. Three numbers, in a single line. The IC needs to know what they're being asked to commit and what's coming back. Anything beyond this line is for the body.

Returns commentary belongs in one short paragraph: where the return comes from, and how robust it is to the obvious shocks. "Base case 2.8x / 22%. Downside (no add-ons, flat multiple) returns 1.6x / 11%. Upside (8 add-ons, multiple expansion to 11x) returns 4.5x / 35%."

Three risks, named

Not "risks include market, execution, and macro". Three specific, deal-particular things that could go wrong, in language a non-specialist could repeat. Customer concentration with a named customer. A regulatory transition with a date. A No.2 who has been in the seat for six months. Specifics. Specifics signal that you've actually thought about the deal.

Diligence findings, not summaries

Three findings, each one in a single sentence. The QoE finding. The commercial finding. The legal/regulatory finding. Not "diligence ongoing." Either you have findings or you should not be in front of the IC.

Deal team and conditions

Who's on the deal. What conditions IC is approving the deal subject to. The conditions are how the IC says yes without saying yes-no-matter-what. Three conditions is normal; six is too many; zero suggests the work isn't done.

The seventh section

The recommendation. One sentence. "The deal team recommends approval, subject to the conditions above." Anything more is throat-clearing.

The IC memo as a forcing function

The discipline of fitting all seven sections on one page is the discipline of knowing what you actually believe. If you cannot describe the thesis in two sentences, you don't have a thesis. If you cannot name three risks, you haven't done diligence. If you cannot list three conditions, you haven't decided what's still uncertain. The memo isn't a deliverable; it's the test.

Build one Open the IC memo builder. Fill in a real (or imagined) deal. Print to PDF. Read it cold. Could you defend each line in front of a partner? If not, that's the work to do before the meeting.
